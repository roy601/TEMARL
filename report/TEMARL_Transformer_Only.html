1. What the model does

The Transformer is the attacker-intent encoder. It reads the sequence of MITRE ATT&CK techniques the attacker has performed so far and produces a single vector h that summarises "what kind of campaign is this, and where is it going". That vector is handed to the four defender agents, which use it to choose which decoy to place and where.

attacker techniques  [T1190, T1059, T1071, ...]

        |
        v
  4 x Transformer encoder layer   (8 heads, feed-forward 512, rotary position)
        |
        v
  last-real-token pooling  ->  LayerNorm  ->   h  (32 dimensions)
        |
        v
  Gated Multimodal Unit  fuses h with network telemetry
        |
        v

The most important finding about this model

An early result reported that the Transformer was beaten on attack prediction by a margin of 0.1388 — a very large, highly significant gap (d = 1.65, p = 5.7×10⁻¹⁴). That result was widely quoted inside the project for two years.

It was not a property of the model. It was our own configuration error. Two fixes — rotary position encoding and an equal tuning budget — closed the entire gap to +0.0000, while the Transformer used 44% of the parameters of the model it had supposedly lost to.

Stated honestly: a trivial frequency baseline scores 0.6835 on this corpus, above the model's 0.6463. COMISET is 98.5% repeated actions, so "say the same thing again" is extremely strong. The model's advantage shows in macro-F1 and top-3, which reward getting the rare, non-repeated transitions right. This is why COMISET was used only for pre-training experiments and never for the thesis's main results.

The model generalises to campaigns it has never seen

On P1 — the hardest possible test, where an entire attack campaign is held out — the Transformer reaches 0.0661 against a majority baseline of 0.0224, an improvement of +0.0477. This is the behaviour attention is expected to show: the advantage appears when the model must generalise to something genuinely new.

The absolute number is small because with only 7 campaigns in the corpus, a held-out campaign shares almost nothing with the training data — roughly 92% of its techniques were never seen during training. That is a property of the dataset, not of the model.

Three things this table establishes

  1. The model beats a defender with no attacker model by +0.0565 containment (p = 0.0004, 10 paired seeds). It is not merely placing decoys — it is placing the right decoys.
  2. It exceeds perfect campaign identification (0.6540 vs 0.6050). Knowing which of three campaign types you face is not enough; the model extracts more than that from the sequence.

On hardware: an Intel Arc GPU was benchmarked and found to be 0.53× the speed of the CPU at the batch size this workload uses (4), because kernel-launch latency dominates at small batches. The GPU only wins at batch 128 or above. All results in this document were produced on CPU, which is the correct choice here.

6. Where the model is weak — stated plainly

  1. It is trained by imitation, not reinforcement learning. Under PPO the model did not beat a fixed-decoy defender at any training budget tried (240 episodes → 0.510; 1,200 → 0.523; baseline 0.5475). The results in this document come from behaviour cloning against an oracle teacher. Why PPO fails here is unresolved.
  2. Prediction accuracy on unseen campaigns is low in absolute terms (0.0661). It beats the baseline by 3×, but the task is very hard with only 7 campaigns.
  3. It is roughly 2× slower to train than a recurrent encoder of the same job, despite being smaller.
  4. A trivial frequency baseline beats it on COMISET top-1. Its advantage there is in macro-F1 and top-3.
  5. The dataset is very small — effective sample size 3.09 independent campaigns. Transformers are designed for large-data regimes.
  6. Sequences are short — 18 to 22 steps. Self-attention's main advantage is modelling long-range dependencies, which barely exist at this length.
  7. Results rest on one environment and one scripted attacker. A learning attacker could change them.

7. Summary

The TEMARL Transformer is a 156,310-parameter attacker-intent encoder that contains attackers 60.4% of the time — 5.7 percentage points better than a defender with no attacker model (p = 0.0004) — capturing 62–70% of what a perfect predictor could achieve, and holding that advantage on network sizes and structures it never saw during training. It predicts genuinely unseen attack campaigns three times better than the baseline. It runs on a CPU in under two minutes per training run.

Two corrections to earlier project claims are recorded here for accuracy: the reported 0.1388 prediction deficit was an artefact of an unsuitable position encoding and an untuned configuration, and was eliminated entirely; and the statement that "the Transformer never won" was incorrect — it led on every condition in the v3 experiment and on the hardest CAM-LDS protocol.
